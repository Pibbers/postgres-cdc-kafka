Production architecture

From 5 tables to 500

The mechanisms don't change with scale. What changes is how much you end up operating — and that is a design choice, not a consequence.

What actually scales

Everything on the live dashboard is the same mechanism that runs at 500 tables: consumer groups, producer operators, landing tables, idempotent merges, TPT jobs. Nothing new appears between 5 and 500. The rows get more numerous, and Teradata absorbs rows well — that is what it is for.

What does change is the size of the estate you operate. And that depends entirely on one decision: whether the number of ingestion jobs grows with the number of tables. In Scenario A it does, exactly one-for-one. In Scenarios B and C it does not.

The whole argument in one line: with Scenario A, every table you add costs you a job. With Scenario B, every table you add costs you a few rows. Both pipelines move the same data — but only one of them makes growth free.

The case for each pattern

A — Job per table

The case for it: it is the only pattern with complete failure isolation. Every table can be tuned, scheduled, prioritised, charged and restarted independently, and the job topology mirrors the schema so anyone can reason about it immediately. For a small, stable estate — roughly 20 tables or fewer — this costs almost nothing and buys real operational clarity. It is a legitimate design, not a strawman, which is why this demo runs it for real.

The case against it at 500: every artefact multiplies by table count. 500 jobs, 500 landing tables, 500 merges, 500 alert rules, 500 runbook entries, and 500 places to touch when a password or a hostname changes. The pipeline's dominant cost stops being the data and becomes the pipeline. Teams that choose this pattern at scale generally end up rebuilding it — usually as Scenario C — once the operational load lands on them.

Verdict: right for small estates, expensive for large ones.

B — One consolidated job

The case for it: job count decouples from table count entirely. One job, one landing table, one merge, one thing to monitor — at 5 tables or 500. Adding a table is a job-definition edit rather than a deployment. The landing table doubles as a raw-payload audit and replay trail. This shape runs comfortably at 8,000–40,000 rows/sec with 100–500ms loader latency, and it is corroborated in production: a logistics operator at 500M messages/day across 17 sources, sustaining 6,200 rows/sec for roughly 0.2% additional CPU; a global automotive manufacturer at 8,000 msg/sec; a telco reference at a billion messages an hour.

The case against it: shared fate is real. One job means one failure domain, one priority and one flush cadence for every table it serves. There is no per-table SLA, so a latency-critical table cannot be favoured. And the merge cadence — not TPT — becomes the dominant latency term, which the merge speed toggle demonstrates live.

Verdict: the default recommendation for a large CDC estate with a uniform SLA.

C — Tiered

The case for it: it removes the false choice. The 10–20 tables that carry a genuine real-time requirement get dedicated jobs with their own consumer groups, merge cadence and workload priority. The remaining several hundred share one or a handful of consolidated jobs. That lands at roughly 20–25 jobs instead of 500 — a 95% reduction in operated surface — while still delivering true isolation exactly where the business needs it. It is also composed entirely of patterns already proven by A and B, so there is nothing novel to validate.

The case against it: it is two patterns to build, document and operate rather than one, and it depends on an honest, maintained answer to "which tables are actually hot?" — a question that is organisational as much as technical. Tier membership drifts, and if the hot list keeps growing you converge back on Scenario A.

Verdict: the right answer whenever SLAs genuinely differ, or isolation is mandated for named tables.

D — Direct multi-table load

The case for it: where the source is already flat and typed, the staging tier has no work to do and can be removed outright. No landing tables, no merge routines, no scheduled downstream component, and the lowest latency of the four because there is no merge cycle to wait for. Most 500-table estates have a meaningful flat share — batch extracts, pre-normalised queue feeds, fixed-schema event streams — and for that share this is simply less to build.

The case against it: it does not apply to CDC change envelopes, which is what most of a 500-table estate typically is. It gives up the staged raw payload, so there is no replay-from-landing safety net. And a single job's target-table count is bounded, so a wide flat estate needs several jobs, each covering a group of tables — still far fewer than one per table, but not a single job for all of them.

Verdict: the right answer for the flat subset of the estate, alongside B or C for the CDC remainder.

Choosing by workload

A mixed estate — most real customers C + D Tier the CDC tables; load the flat feeds direct. The two compose cleanly.

The recommended shape at 500 tables

Start from consolidation and add isolation only where it is earned. For a typical 500-table CDC estate that means:

  • 10–30 CDC connectors grouped by business domain, consolidating into 1–3 Kafka topics — topic count does not need to mirror table count either
  • 1 consolidated TPT job for the warm and cold tail, with one landing table and one fan-out merge (Scenario B)
  • Dedicated jobs for the 10–20 tables with a genuine real-time SLA, each on its own workload tier (Scenario C)
  • Direct multi-table loads for whatever share of the estate arrives already flat (Scenario D)
  • Pilot with 10–20 representative tables first — validate latency, monitoring and workload management, then scale out. The architecture does not change between the pilot and the full estate, which is the point of choosing it this way

Net: roughly 20–25 jobs to operate rather than 500, with real-time isolation exactly where the business requires it and consolidated economics everywhere else.

The close

Everything on the dashboard — job counts, resource footprint, landing latency, the isolation trade-off on stop and restart — is the same mechanism that runs at 500 tables. The only thing that changes between 5 and 500 is which pattern you choose for which tables. Choose well and the estate grows without the operating cost growing with it.
